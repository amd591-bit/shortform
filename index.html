Course Goals


1. Match Text to Visuals

I learned how to pair typography and visuals so that my layouts and colors enhance written messaging rather than cluttering it on my projects. Through the Good Design / Design Principles Study project, I applied core visual principles—such as contrast, alignment, proximity, space, and repetition— learned from Non-Designers book- to ensure my text and imagery balanced each other seamlessly.

2. Master the InDesign Interface and Workspace.

Getting comfortable with InDesign took some practice, but I learned how to set up layers, navigate the workspace, and organize panels to work faster. Projects like the Ten-Page Spread and the Infographic really helped me get the hang of managing multi-page documents without getting lost in the software.

3. Manage Images and Vector Graphics

I got a lot of practice placing, cropping, and scaling graphics without losing image quality. Working on my initial Logo A/B/C ideas and building out the final UX Testing Logo taught me how to work with vector shapes and export graphics properly for real-world use.



4. Build Technical Resilience and Troubleshooting Habits

Whenever something broke—like text overflowing or layout code not aligning—I learned not to panic and instead figure out what went wrong. Building my UX Design Branding Project and coding this Portfolio Website pushed me to troubleshoot bugs, use online resources, and keep tweaking until everything looked right.

5. Manage Time and Project Workflows Efficiently

I realized quickly that design projects can fall behind if you don't plan ahead for revisions. For the UX Testing Logo Project, I gathered peer feedback and user interview notes early on, which gave me enough time to refine my ideas and turn in a strong final design on schedule.

Short Form


Two-page Spread

A magazine spread exploring how constantly checking our phones impacts our attention spans and daily lives. I used bold typography and structured grid layouts to keep the design clean while making the topic visually engaging.

Infographic

A visual guide breaking down how our bodies process carbs, fats, and proteins. My main goal here was to take complex nutritional science and make it easy to digest through clear visual hierarchy and color coding.

Advertisement

A print-style ad for a protein powder, focusing on warm tones and clean spacing. I worked on balancing strong, persuasive headline copy with clear product details to reach a health-conscious audience.

Accessibile Design

A practical guide on building digital content that works for everyone. It highlights simple design choices—like high-contrast colors, clear alt-text, and screen-reader-friendly layouts—that make a big difference for neurodiverse and visually impaired users.

Course Goals


1. Consider the role of rhetoric, technology, and ethics in acts of making.

Designing these projects forced me to think beyond just making things look good. I used rhetoric to shape tone and persuade—whether that was picking the right words for the protein ad or structuring the argument in the magazine spread. On the technical side, I brought these ideas to life using tools like InDesign alongside web code like HTML and CSS. Ethics came into play most when building the Accessible Design guide, which reminded me that good design needs to be inclusive for everyone, including neurodiverse readers and people using screen readers.

2. Leverage Design Principles.

Core principles like visual hierarchy, color theory, contrast, and layout grids were essential to keeping my work organized. For the Macronutrients Infographic, I used color coding and distinct sections so readers could easily follow the flow of information. For the advertisement, I focused on high contrast and bold typography to grab attention right away without cluttering the page.

3. Engage with others in a workshop environment.

Feedback from my peers made a massive difference in how these projects turned out. During workshop sessions, getting fresh eyes on my drafts helped me spot layout issues, awkward contrast, or messy typography that I had missed. The feedback on early versions of my Infographic and Accessible Design guide helped me streamline the reading order and clean up visual clutter before finalizing them.



4. Practice revision within and across media forms.

This portfolio was a lesson in constant editing and adapting content across different mediums. Designing a fixed print layout for a magazine spread is very different from translating those same visual pieces into a responsive web page using code (index.html). Going back and forth between static layouts and web code required multiple rounds of tweaking, recoding, and rearranging elements until everything felt cohesive.

5. Learn to present your work.

Putting this entire web portfolio together gave me real-world practice in showcasing my work effectively. Instead of just dropping images onto a page, I set up interactive modals, structured cards, and written project captions to give context to each piece. It taught me how to present my projects thoughtfully while showing off both my visual design choices and my front-end coding skills.
